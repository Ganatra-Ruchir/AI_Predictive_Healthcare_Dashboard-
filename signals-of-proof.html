Why it matters

The gap wearables are built to fill

Long-term illnesses — heart disease, cancer, diabetes, lung disease — cause 74 out of every 100 deaths worldwide. [1] These are slow, quiet conditions that a once-a-year doctor's visit is poorly built to catch. That's the gap AI-enabled wearables are being asked to fill — and regulators already treat it as real medicine: over 1,000 AI-powered medical devices were officially approved in the U.S. by the end of 2024. [2]

01

Heart

Catching a dangerous irregular heartbeat early

Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is an irregular heartbeat that can come and go with no symptoms at all — yet it's one of the top causes of stroke that could otherwise be prevented. This is the best-studied use of AI in wearables, and it's also the clearest lesson that "accurate" and "actually helpful" are two different things.

AI performance

Can a smartwatch tell AFib apart from a normal heartbeat?

In plain terms: if you take 100 people who truly have AFib and check them with a smartwatch, it correctly spots about 95 of them. And if you take 100 people with a normal heartbeat, it correctly clears about 97 of them.

100 people with AFib → 95 correctly caught

100 people without AFib → 3 wrongly flagged

These two numbers have names doctors use: how often it catches a real case is called sensitivity, and how often it correctly clears a healthy person is called specificity. That's from a 2025 review pooling 26 studies and 17,349 patients — the largest look at this question yet, and a bigger, newer confirmation of what an earlier 2025 review of 11 studies had already found (94.8% / 95.0%).

It also breaks results down by brand — useful if you're wondering whether the watch on your wrist specifically has been tested:

Apple Watch94% / 97%
Samsung Galaxy Watch97% / 96%
Withings89% / 95%

Numbers shown are sensitivity / specificity for each brand. All three land in a similar, reassuring range.

One catch: the 26 studies didn't all agree with each other nearly as much as you'd hope — a sign researchers call heterogeneity was very high for this pooled result, and the earlier, smaller 11-study review found several studies had weaker designs too (7 of 11 had a higher chance of bias in how patients were picked or timed).

Newest: 26 studies, 17,349 patients, multiple smartwatch brands (2025) [N2] · Earlier: 11 studies, 4,241 people, Apple Watch only, AUC 0.96 — Shahid et al., 2025 [4]

Real-world test

Does catching AFib early actually help in real life?

In a real trial with 437 older adults already at high risk of stroke, people who wore a monitoring smartwatch for six months were much more likely to have a hidden AFib episode caught, compared to people who just got their usual check-ups.

Wore the watch — 219 people21 caught (9.6%)
Usual care — 218 people5 caught (2.3%)

Wearing the watch found roughly 4 times more hidden AFib cases. Put another way: for about every 14 high-risk people who wear a monitoring watch for six months, one extra dangerous case gets caught that would likely have been missed otherwise.

EQUAL randomized trial · adults 65+ at elevated stroke risk · 6 months · HR 4.40 · van Steijn et al., 2026 [5]

The cost of false alarms

What happens when the watch gets it wrong?

Out of every 15 alerts a monitoring watch sent in one study, about 10 turned out to be false alarms — no AFib was actually found when doctors checked with a more thorough test.

Of 15 people alerted, 10 (about 2 out of 3) had no AFib on a same-day follow-up test.

These false alarms weren't harmless — people who got one reported feeling measurably worse physically and less confident managing their health afterward, the more false alarms they had.

Pulsewatch study · 85 people, history of stroke or mini-stroke · Tran et al., 2023 [14]

Metabolism

Steadier blood sugar, and the limits of forecasting

Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) replace occasional finger-prick tests with constant, automatic readings. Two different questions matter here: does the constant monitoring itself help, and can a computer model reliably guess what your blood sugar will do next?

The monitor helps — not the AI

Does constant monitoring actually improve blood sugar?

Yes. People who used a CGM instead of finger-prick testing ended up with noticeably steadier blood sugar over time, across 12 separate real-world trials.

no change

This measures HbA1c, the main blood-sugar score doctors track. A drop of 0.31 points sounds tiny, but on this scale it's a real, meaningful improvement — not just noise. Credit the constant monitoring itself here, not an AI prediction.

A much bigger 2026 review — 30 trials, 3,133 people — confirms this and finds CGM helps in more ways than blood sugar alone:

The one thing it did not show a clear difference in: actual heart-attack or stroke events, or dangerous low-blood-sugar episodes — those need longer, larger trials to settle.

Original finding: 12 randomized trials, 1,248 adults — Jancev et al., 2024 [8] · Newer, bigger confirmation: 30 trials, 3,133 adults (2026) [N5]

AI performance

Can AI predict your blood sugar before it happens?

A little, and only for a short while ahead — like a weather forecast. The further into the future it tries to guess, the less accurate the guess gets.

Guessing 60 minutes ahead was +139% less accurate than guessing just 15 minutes ahead (our arithmetic on their reported numbers). No single AI model was best in every study — so this isn't ready to run on its own without a person checking in.

10 studies · error measured in RMSE (mg/dL) · 15.02 → 21.49 → 30.09 → 35.89 · Kapoor & Hasija, 2025 [9]

Movement

Parkinson's disease — exciting ideas, still small proof

Wearable AI for Parkinson's disease is a busy, fast-moving research area right now. Three recent studies show three very different levels of real-world proof — and the difference matters.

Check who was actually tested

A "100% accurate" AI — tested on how many people?

Just 9 people. A perfect score sounds impressive, but on a group this small it doesn't yet tell us how the AI would perform on anyone else.

Correct on its test group100%

8 people with Parkinson's plus 1 healthy volunteer — that's the entire study. Even the researchers who built it call this an early proof-of-concept, not a clinical result.

Motigravity motion-sensor platform, hybrid CNN-LSTM model · Benyoucef et al., 2025 [E5]

The headline number vs. the harder test

Does it still work on someone the AI has never met?

A wearable ankle sensor that detects "freezing" episodes (a sudden inability to move that raises fall risk) scored well on its own test group — but did noticeably worse when checked on a brand-new patient.

Tested on people it already knew94.9%
Tested on a brand-new patient81.4%

Reacting in under a second is genuinely useful — but this gap is why the harder, more realistic test matters more than the headline number.

ParCuR ankle wearable, SVM model, 0.7s reaction time · Lopes et al., 2025 [E6]

Looking across the whole field

Across 66 studies, how many were tested the hard, realistic way?

Almost none. Out of 66 Parkinson's wearable-AI studies reviewed, only 4 tested their AI on a completely new group of patients — the strongest kind of test there is.

Tested on a brand-new group of patients ⓘ4 of 66
Tested one patient at a time, rest for training ⓘ37 of 66
Split the same group into chunks ⓘ27 of 66

This is the single clearest gap in the whole page: most Parkinson's wearable-AI studies haven't yet been tested the way that would prove they work on someone new.

66 studies, ~3,579 participants — Li et al., 2026 scoping review [E8]

Mind

Spotting early memory and thinking decline

Can a wearable's activity and sleep data help catch early signs of dementia? 49 studies have looked into it — and the honest picture is a huge combined pool of people, made up of many small individual studies.

Here's the catch: 200,000+ people sounds huge, but the typical single study only followed about 145 people — and most used research-only sensors, not the smartwatches people actually wear day to day.

87.8% of studies used research-grade sensors rather than the commercial wearables people actually own, and only about a quarter followed people over months or years to test real early-warning prediction — most just compared two groups at a single point in time.

One more honest detail: despite all the "AI" headlines, almost three-quarters (36 of 49) of these studies just used traditional statistics, not machine learning. Only 7 used classic machine learning and 6 used deep learning — so most of today's dementia-detection evidence isn't actually "AI" yet, it's careful counting.

49 studies, published Jan 2020–Dec 2025 · Cejudo et al., 2026 [E7]

05

Seizures

Great at noticing — barely tried at predicting

Epilepsy causes sudden seizures that can strike without warning. Wearable AI is being built to watch for them — but a look across 67 studies shows the field is almost entirely focused on noticing a seizure as it happens, not warning someone before it starts.

What is this AI actually trying to do?

Spot it happening, or predict it in advance?

Out of 67 studies on wearable seizure-tracking AI, 4 in 5 were built to notice a seizure already in progress — hardly any tried to warn someone before one starts.

Detects a seizure as it happens54 of 67
Tries to predict one before it starts14 of 67

A true early warning — the kind that could let someone sit down or take medication in time — is still the exception, not the rule.

67 studies, wearable seizure-tracking evidence map — 2025 [N11]

Some of the strongest numbers in this whole review

How accurately can AI spot a seizure from brainwaves?

Extremely accurately — 96 out of 100 real seizures caught, and 97 out of 100 non-seizure moments correctly left alone — combining 60 studies and 93 datasets of brain-wave (EEG) recordings.

Sensitivity (catches real seizures)96%
Specificity (leaves non-seizures alone)97%

The catch: this is reading brainwaves directly in a lab (EEG), not yet the everyday wrist or ear wearable most people would use — and real wrist/ear devices, while promising, still struggle with false alarms as their central challenge, the same lesson as the Heart chapter above.

60 studies, 93 EEG datasets — meta-analysis, 2025 [N12] · Wrist/ear wearable synthesis, 2026 [N13]

Can other researchers check this work?

Most of this research can't be independently checked

Nearly 2 in 3 studies (44 of 67) used private data that no other research team can access or re-test.

Private, closed data means other scientists can't double-check the results or test the model on their own patients — one reason results that look great in one study don't always hold up elsewhere.

67 studies, wearable seizure-tracking evidence map — 2025 [N11]

06

Sleep

One of the biggest studies in this whole review

Can a simple wearable screen for sleep apnea — a common condition where breathing repeatedly stops during sleep? One 2026 study answering this question covers 23,171 people, making it the largest single piece of evidence anywhere on this page.

AI performance, at scale

Can a finger oxygen sensor screen for sleep apnea?

Yes, reasonably well: out of 100 people who truly have sleep apnea, it correctly catches about 91. Out of 100 who don't, it correctly clears about 88.

100 people with sleep apnea → 91 caught

100 people without → 12 wrongly flagged

Using just an overnight fingertip oxygen sensor (no brain-wave headgear needed). The best-performing models — neural networks — did even better: about 93 caught, 91 cleared.

25 studies, 23,171 participants — Bayesian meta-analysis, 2026 [N16]

A textbook sensitivity/specificity tradeoff

When "catches almost everyone" has a cost

A different oximetry study catches 97 of 100 true cases — but wrongly flags 37 of every 100 healthy sleepers.

With sleep apnea → 97 caught

Without → 37 wrongly flagged

This is the sensitivity/specificity tradeoff in action: a device tuned to almost never miss a real case ends up wrongly worrying a lot of healthy people, who would then need a follow-up sleep study to sort it out.

18 studies, oximetry-based wearables vs. full sleep study [N18]

What strong validation actually looks like

A device tested the right way — on people it had never seen

A compact fingertip sleep sensor was built and tuned on 240 people, then tested again on 112 completely different, independent people — and its readings still closely matched a full hospital sleep study.

Agreement with hospital-grade breathing count0.95 correlation
Agreement on how severe the case was0.70 (substantial)

This is exactly the kind of test the Movement chapter above found almost totally missing for Parkinson's wearables (only 4 of 66 studies) — proof that testing a device on brand-new people, not just the ones it was built with, is achievable when researchers choose to do it.

352 participants total (240 to build it, 112 independent to test it) — TipTraQ device, prospective validation, 2025 [N17]

Mind & Mood

Detecting depression works. Predicting it is much harder.

Wearables can pick up on sleep, activity and heart-rate patterns that shift with mental health. Detecting depression that's already present works surprisingly well — forecasting a future episode, or "how stressed am I really," is a different story.

Detecting depression, right now

Can a wearable tell if someone is currently depressed?

Yes, quite well: 89 of 100 real cases caught, 93 of 100 healthy people correctly cleared, combining 16 studies.

Sensitivity89%
Specificity93%

People with depression also show up as measurably different on wearables: about 32 minutes more time in bed, slightly later sleep onset, and noticeably lower physical activity than people without it — across a separate synthesis of 132 studies.

Detection: 16 studies / 32 datasets, 2026 [N19] · Biomarkers: 132 studies, 2026 [N20]

Predicting a future episode

Can it warn you before an episode happens?

Much less reliably: sensitivity drops only a little (86%), but specificity falls to 65% — meaning roughly 1 in 3 healthy stretches get wrongly flagged as a coming episode.

Catches a real coming episode86%
Correctly says "nothing coming"65%

The same pattern shows up everywhere in this review: telling what's happening right now is a solved problem far more often than telling what's about to happen. Personalized, one-person-at-a-time models tend to do better than one-size-fits-all ones, but studies lasting a full year or more are still rare.

3 prediction datasets within the same 2026 review [N19] · Longitudinal passive-sensing review, 2025 [N21]

Be skeptical of numbers that sound too perfect

"99% accurate at detecting stress" — should you believe it?

Be cautious. A review of wearable stress-detection research found some individual studies claiming accuracy up to 99% — but flagged that many of those same studies used very small groups of people, inconsistent ways of measuring "stress" in the first place, and no standard testing protocol.

This isn't unique to stress research — it's a pattern worth remembering anywhere in AI healthcare: an eye-catching headline number is only as trustworthy as the study design behind it. Separate reviews of workplace stress (20 studies) and student stress (134 studies, dominated by skin-conductance sensors) found the same small-sample, inconsistent-methods pattern.

Stress prediction review, 2025 [N22] · Workplace stress, 2026 [N23] · College stress sensing, 2026 [N25]

Breathing

COPD — frequent check-ins, not yet true 24/7 monitoring

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) can suddenly get worse — a "flare-up" that often lands someone in hospital if not caught early. Can a wearable warn of one coming?

Partly. Across 26 studies, 20 tried to detect or predict a flare-up early, mainly by tracking heart rate, blood-oxygen level, or simply asking people to log their symptoms.

Heart rate tracked9 of 26 studies
Blood-oxygen level tracked9 of 26 studies
Symptom check-ins used8 of 26 studies

One honest limit: most studies (14 of 26) only checked in daily or weekly, not continuously — so this is closer to a frequent check-in than true around-the-clock monitoring.

26 studies, wearable/remote COPD monitoring — 2026 [N26]

Frontier

Two big ideas that aren't ready yet

These two ideas get talked about constantly. Across seven separate research reviews, the verdict is the same for both: real, active research — but rarely used in actual patient care yet.

Federated learning

Teaching AI without sharing anyone's private data

Federated learning lets hospitals train one shared AI together without ever sending a single patient's private records anywhere else. It's a great privacy idea — but it's mostly still on the drawing board.

Out of 612 studies on this idea, only about 5 in every 100 (highlighted above) were actually tried in a real hospital setting — the rest were lab experiments or computer simulations.

Human digital twins

A living computer copy of you

A digital twin is a personalized simulation that updates itself as new health data comes in. It's a compelling idea that's still mostly a research concept, not something your doctor can use yet.

Out of 149 studies calling themselves a "digital twin," only about 12 in every 100 (highlighted above) fully met the strict scientific definition — and just 2 studies even checked how much to trust their own simulation's results.

Aging & Frailty

Reading today's frailty is easier than forecasting tomorrow's

Frailty — the gradual loss of strength and resilience in older adults — is a strong predictor of hospital stays and falls. Can AI models spot it, and predict how it will change?

Today's snapshot vs. tomorrow's trajectory

Predicting frailty now is easier than predicting its future

Across 45 prediction models, telling how frail someone is right now works well — predicting how it will change over time is noticeably harder.

Accuracy — frailty at a single point in time0.878
Accuracy — how frailty changes over time0.730

This is the same "detection is easier than forecasting" pattern seen in glucose predictions, seizure prediction and depression episodes above — a recurring honest limit of wearable AI generally, not a one-off weak spot.

45 models, 10 studies — meta-analysis, 2025 [N29]

A small silver lining

Is fancier AI actually better here?

Not clearly. A separate review of 181 frailty studies found little evidence that complex AI models outperform simpler statistical ones — and classic, cross-sectional (single check-up) study designs still dominate the field.

Models tested on a completely new group of people9 of 45

That 1-in-5 external-validation rate is actually better than most other domains in this review (Parkinson's managed only 1 in 16) — a sign this field, while not flashy, is being reasonably careful.

181 articles, frailty-modeling methodology review — 2025 [N30]

Equity

Who actually gets to benefit

If hospitals rely on the wearable you already own, then whoever can't afford or access one gets left out of the benefit too. Of nearly 24,000 people surveyed in the U.S., fewer than half (44.5%) owned any wearable device at all. [13]

In plain terms: older adults and people in rural areas are much less likely to own a wearable — while wealthier people are much more likely to. The bars below show how far below or above "average" each group is (the middle line).

A separate, widely cited study found a hospital algorithm was quietly less likely to flag Black patients for extra care — not because they were healthier, but because the algorithm used how much money had been spent on their care as a stand-in for how sick they were. Once that one design choice was fixed, the unfairness disappeared. [15] That wasn't even a wearables study — but it's the same lesson: a well-meaning AI can still be unfair if nobody checks its assumptions.

Guardrails

The rules being built to keep this safe

An AI device can quietly change after it's approved — unlike a pill, which is always the same. So regulators are building a new kind of rulebook for it. Here's what's happened since June 2024, in order.

Jun 2024

Transparency guiding principles

FDA, Health Canada & MHRA jointly publish nonbinding principles on communicating intended use, performance, and limitations. [16]

Dec 2024

1,016 authorized AI/ML devices

FDA's authorized-device list reaches four figures — with the agency itself noting it isn't exhaustive. [2]

Jan 2025

Draft: AI lifecycle management

Lifecycle-management and marketing-submission recommendations for AI device software functions. [R1]

Jun 2025

Final: medical-device cybersecurity

Quality-system and premarket cybersecurity expectations for connected devices. [R2]

Aug 2025

Final: pre-approved update plans

Lets a manufacturer say upfront, in writing, exactly how its AI device is allowed to update itself later — instead of needing a brand-new approval every time. [17]

2025

AMA policy on liability

Responsibility should sit with whoever is best positioned to understand, avert, or mitigate a given risk. [18]

Jan 2026

Final: General Wellness policy

Distinguishes low-risk wellness functions from regulated medical-device claims. [R4]

Jan 2026

Final: Clinical Decision Support Software

Jul 2026

Scorecard

What to feel good about — and what still needs proving

This is worth being genuinely proud of: real trials on real patients showing real effects. It's also worth staying clear-eyed — that honesty is what makes the "proven" column trustworthy.

Proven
  • Smartwatch ECG discriminates AFib at 95% sensitivity / 97% specificity, confirmed across 26 studies and 17,349 patients.
  • An integrated smartwatch pathway more than quadrupled AFib detection in a randomized trial of high-risk older adults.
  • CGM meaningfully improves HbA1c, time-in-range, weight and blood pressure across 30+ randomized trials.
  • A fingertip oxygen sensor can screen for sleep apnea, validated on 23,171 people — and one study proved this properly on 112 brand-new patients.
  • Detecting depression from wearable data, or a seizure from brainwaves, both work with 89–97% accuracy.
Promising, incomplete
  • Gait-cueing wearables for Parkinson's work in real time, but accuracy drops on patients the model hasn't seen.
  • Dementia detection has a huge combined evidence base, but it's mostly plain statistics, not AI, and rarely tracks people over time.
  • Predicting the future — glucose in 60 minutes, a depression episode, tomorrow's frailty — is consistently harder than detecting what's true right now, in every domain reviewed.
  • Sleep-apnea screening that catches almost every real case also wrongly flags over a third of healthy people in some studies — a real sensitivity/specificity tradeoff, not a solved problem.
Early-stage
  • Federated learning — four independent 2024–26 reviews, all finding real-world deployment rare.
  • Human digital twins — only 12% of studies meet the formal definition, across three separate reviews.
  • A 9-participant "100% accurate" model is a headline, not a clinical result — sample size is everything.
  • Wearable seizure research is almost entirely detection, not prediction, and two-thirds of it uses private data no one else can check.
  • Some "99% accurate" stress-detection claims come from small, inconsistently designed studies — treat eye-catching numbers with real caution.

Read this before you get too excited

  • The EQUAL trial shows more AF gets detected — it does not yet show fewer strokes or lower mortality.
  • Zero evidence rows in this expansion are confirmed to use strictly 2025–26 collected patient data — publication year isn't collection year.
  • Every headline accuracy number describes one sensor, task, and population — not a universal "AI is X% accurate."
  • Most Parkinson's, dementia, FL, and digital-twin studies here are small samples or proof-of-concept, not deployment-ready systems.
